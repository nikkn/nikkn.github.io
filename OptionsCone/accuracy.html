Accuracy

How accurate are the realized odds?

OptionsCone shows two realized models next to the option prices: 10y and adaptive. We tested both against what actually happened, together with the textbook formula, the standard way to turn a volatility into probabilities. It is an out-of-sample test: on every test date, each model only saw the data up to that day, and its predictions were then compared with what happened afterwards. This page shows the results, including where the models are weak.

Summary

  • Adaptive came closest in every group: stocks, ETFs, and gold and silver. For stocks, its predictions at expiry missed what happened by 0.27 points on average, against 1.49 for 10y and 2.41 for the textbook formula. For touch: 0.73, 2.53 and 2.48.
  • 10y beats the textbook formula at expiry, but not for touch. Its long-run average misses how differently a stock moves in calm and in turbulent phases. Adaptive adds today's conditions and stays close in both.
  • The 90% range holds: 89.9% of real closing prices ended inside adaptive's 90% range for stocks, 90.3% for ETFs and 90.2% for gold and silver.
  • The gain is largest when volatility is far from normal. In calm markets, 10y missed the touch odds of stocks by 11.0 points on average, adaptive by 1.7. In turbulent markets, 10y missed by 6.1 points, the textbook formula by 5.9 and adaptive by 0.5.
  • There are weak spots, and they are listed below: gold and silver rose more than every model expected, far ETF puts were reached a little less often than predicted, and the stocks are today's S&P 500 members.

What was tested

On each test date, every model only saw the data up to that day. It then said how likely the price was to touch a strike before expiry, and how likely it was to finish beyond the strike at expiry. We compared that with what the price did over the following weeks and months.

  • Instruments: 448 S&P 500 stocks with at least nine years of earnings history, 12 ETFs (SPY, QQQ, IWM and the sector ETFs XLB, XLE, XLF, XLI, XLK, XLP, XLU, XLV, XLY), gold and silver (GLD, SLV).
  • Test dates: January 2016 to April 2026, every 21 trading days for stocks and every 5 for ETFs, gold and silver. 57,876 test dates in total: 50,663 for stocks, 6,240 for ETFs and 973 for gold and silver.
  • Terms: 2 weeks, 1 month, 2, 3 and 6 months.
  • Strikes: calls and puts at four distances from the price, from half to two times the typical move over the term.
  • Models: adaptive, 10y, and the textbook formula, a random walk at today's volatility without an expected return.
  • Data and model: daily prices, dividends and earnings dates from Yahoo Finance. The models are tested with the same model code as in OptionsCone 1.4.
  • Ranges: the 90% ranges on this page account for the overlap of test periods: they come from resampling whole half-years of test dates.

Each model made 2,026,520 predictions for stocks, 249,600 for ETFs and 38,920 for gold and silver, for touch and again for expiry. That is about 4.6 million predictions per model and almost 14 million in total, each one compared with what happened.

For statisticians

Show the details: Brier score and its parts, calibration in the large, slope, CRPS, and said vs happened by probability level

Brier score (×100, lower is better) and skill against 10y and against the textbook formula (in %, higher is better), with 90% ranges. The Murphy parts (×100): reliability is the calibration error (lower is better), resolution is how well a model separates likely from unlikely (higher is better), uncertainty is the part no model can remove. Calibration in the large is the average of said minus happened (points, 0 is right). The slope is 1 when the odds are neither too extreme nor too timid.

CRPS scores the whole forecast distribution, here for the closing price, the highest and the lowest price over the term. Lower is better. The difference to 10y and to the textbook formula is in %, with its 90% range.

Most of a Brier score is chance that no model can remove, so skill scores of a few percent are what good calibration looks like here. The calibration part (reliability) is where the models differ most.

Weak spots and caveats

These are the weak spots the test found, and the limits of the test itself. All numbers are said minus happened or average misses, in percentage points.

  • Gold and silver rose more than every model expected. At expiry, all three models said too low for calls (adaptive by 3.2 points, 10y by 3.8, the textbook formula by 7.8) and too high for puts. The closing price ended above adaptive's 95% line in 7.5% of cases instead of 5%. This was a strong rally, measured on only two instruments and 973 test dates, so the ranges are wide.
  • In calm phases, the textbook formula came closer on gold and silver. Its average miss at expiry was 2.45 points, against 3.68 for adaptive, on 276 test dates.
  • Far ETF puts, two to six months out, were reached a little less often than all models predicted: adaptive by 1.0 point, 10y by 1.7 and the textbook formula by 4.0. After sell-offs, the drops often did not continue.
  • Stocks in turbulent phases rebounded slightly more than adaptive expected. At expiry, calls finished beyond the strike 0.7 points more often than predicted, puts 1.3 points less often.
  • In a few subgroups another model came closer: ETFs from 2021 to 2026 for touch, where 10y missed by 1.17 points and adaptive by 1.76. ETFs for touch in normal volatility and inside the reliability zone, where the textbook formula was closer. Gold and silver for touch in normal volatility, where 10y was closer.
  • The reliability zone does not sort the probabilities. Adaptive was as close outside the zone as inside (stocks, touch: 0.77 points outside, 1.25 inside). In the app, the zone limits the expected value and risk maps, where far strikes and high volatility matter most.
  • Survivorship: the stocks are today's S&P 500 members, so companies that left the index are missing. The ETFs have no such gap and show the same picture.
  • Overlapping test periods: test dates every 5 or 21 trading days with terms up to six months overlap. The 90% ranges account for this by resampling whole half-years, but with 21 half-years they are themselves only approximate.
  • The past is not the future: the test covers 2016 to 2026, terms up to six months and strikes up to two standard deviations from the price. Other periods, longer terms and farther strikes were not tested.

All numbers count what happened in the past. They describe how often events happened over millions of predictions, so single trades can turn out very differently. They are not probabilities for the future, not forecasts and not financial advice.
