AMD wheel strategy: twice the swings, yet assigned shares came back more often

By Nikolai Alexander · 19 November 2026

The wheel on AMD runs in three steps: sell a put, take the shares if you are assigned, then sell covered calls until the shares are called away. This guide shows how each step went for AMD on every trading day of the last ten years, next to the average S&P 500 stock.

AMD moves about twice as much as the average S&P 500 stock. Over 30 days, one typical move was about 16.2% at its usual volatility of 52% over the last ten years. It reports earnings four times a year, and on report day the stock moved a median 7.5%, more than twice the 3.2% of the average stock. In late October 2026 its volatility over the previous three months was about 60%, which puts one typical move at roughly 19%.

A typical move is the stock's volatility over the previous three months, scaled to 30 days. All strikes in this guide sit a number of typical moves from the price, so the distances follow AMD's own swings instead of a fixed percentage.

Step 1: selling the put

The wheel starts with a sold put, usually a cash secured put. If AMD stays above the strike, the put expires worthless and you keep the premium. The table shows how often that happened for 30-day puts.

Measured in its own typical moves, AMD's puts expired worthless about as often as the average stock's close to the price, and a little more often further out. In percent, the strikes sat about twice as far away.

Earnings: a report inside the 30 days lowered the share a little, less than for the average stock. One typical move below the price, 89% of AMD's puts expired worthless with a report and 90% without. For the average stock it was 85% and 88%.

Touched before expiry: about one put in four at one typical move was touched before expiry (24%). Of those, 57% still expired worthless, against 54% for the average stock, so a touch alone did not decide the trade.

Step 2: if you get assigned

When a put one typical move below the price finished in the money, AMD closed a median 6.1% below the strike at expiry, and in one case in ten more than 15% below it. For the average stock it was 3.5% and 13%.

The question for the wheel is how long the shares take to get back to the strike, the price you paid for them. The table shows how often AMD closed at or above the strike at least once within 30, 60 and 90 days after the assignment.

Assigned AMD shares fell further below the strike than those of the average stock, but they also got back to it clearly more often: nine in ten within 90 days. About one put in ten finished in the money, so these numbers rest on about 250 assignments over ten years. That makes them less certain than the numbers for the average stock, but the gap of 13 to 17 points held at all three horizons.

Step 3: the covered call

With the shares in the account, the wheel continues with covered calls. If AMD stays below the strike, the call expires worthless, you keep the premium and the shares. The table shows how often that happened for 30-day calls.

AMD's calls expired worthless less often than the average stock's, most clearly further from the price. Earnings widened the gap: one typical move above the price, 70% of the calls expired worthless when a report fell inside the 30 days, and 80% without one. For the average stock it was 77% and 85%.

When the shares were called away, AMD closed a median 7.8% above the strike at expiry, and in one case in ten more than 28% above it. That gain goes to the buyer of the call. For the average stock it was 2.9% and 10%. This is where AMD's large swings cost a covered call seller the most.

Data and limits

AMD daily closing, high and low prices for the last ten years, November 2016 to October 2026, adjusted for splits. Every trading day is a start date (2,493 starts). 30 days means 21 trading days. Worthless means the close on the last day was on the safe side of the strike. Touched means the daily high or low reached it. The average stock pools 469 of today's S&P 500 members over the same years.

  • AMD rose strongly over these ten years, and the average stock is made of today's index members, so both look back on a history of winners. A strong uptrend favors puts and quick recoveries after an assignment, works against covered calls, and the coming years need not look the same.
  • The strikes are set with the stock's past volatility. Implied volatility is usually higher, so a strike one typical move away by the implied volatility your platform shows sits a little further out.
  • All numbers are shares of many past trades, not a prediction for a single trade. AMD's numbers also come from one stock only and are less certain than those of the average stock.

AMD wheel FAQ

How often do AMD puts expire worthless?

From November 2016 to October 2026, 90% of 30-day AMD puts one typical move below the price expired worthless, about 14% below the price at AMD's usual volatility. For the average S&P 500 stock it was 87%. Half a typical move below the price it was 76%, two typical moves below it 99%.

How long did AMD take to get back to the strike after an assignment?

Of the puts one typical move below the price that finished in the money, AMD closed at or above the strike again within 30 days in 75% of cases, within 60 days in 86% and within 90 days in 90%. For the average S&P 500 stock it was 58%, 70% and 77%.

Do covered calls on AMD get called away often?

More often than on the average stock. One typical move above the price, 77% of AMD's 30-day calls expired worthless, against 83% for the average S&P 500 stock, and only 70% when an earnings report fell inside the 30 days. When the shares were called away, AMD closed a median 7.8% above the strike, and in one case in ten more than 28% above it.
