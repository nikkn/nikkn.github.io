Methods: what the numbers mean

What OptionsCone shows, where the numbers come from, and where their limits are.

Data

Option chains, stock prices and earnings dates come from Yahoo Finance. Option quotes are delayed by about 15 minutes. Every download is archived on your computer. The chart uses the newest download taken during the regular US session, when option prices are meaningful.

Interest rates come from US Treasury bill yields. Future dividends are projected from the stock's payout history.

Which contracts are shown

Calls above the current price and puts below it, so every contract on the chart is out of the money. Those are the liquid quotes. Contracts very close to expiry, and contracts whose price is only a few cents, are left out, because their prices say little.

Every quote passes a series of plausibility checks. Impossible quotes are removed. Doubtful ones stay on the chart, but their dot is drawn hollow and the data quality report counts them.

Implied volatility and Greeks

Listed US stock options are American, so each contract's implied volatility is solved with a model that allows early exercise and accounts for dividends. Delta and gamma come from the same model and are shown for comparison with a broker. Brokers use their own rate and dividend assumptions, so small differences are normal.

Probability of touch

The probability that the price reaches a strike at any time before expiry, even if it moves back afterwards. It uses each contract's own implied volatility. A touch probability is never lower than the probability at expiry, and often about twice as high.

Probability at expiry

The probability that the price finishes beyond the strike on expiry day, with the same volatility and assumptions as the touch probability, so the two are directly comparable.

Delta is not a probability

Delta is often read as the chance of an option expiring in the money. For short-dated options at moderate volatility the two are close. The higher the volatility and the longer the time to expiry, the further they drift apart: a 30-delta put on a volatile stock several months out can have a noticeably higher chance of expiring in the money than 30%. Delta remains the right number for hedging. For odds, use the probability at expiry, or the touch probability if the question is whether a level will be reached.

How large the gap is in real option chains, and how the skew changes it, is shown in the study Is delta the probability of expiring in the money?

Realized odds (10y, adaptive)

The realized odds answer the same questions from the stock's own moves instead of option prices. 10y uses industry-standard probability models, built on the stock's realized moves of the last ten years, including calm and turbulent periods and intraday moves. Adaptive takes the same models and makes them forward-looking: adjusted to each stock's current conditions, the events ahead and how this particular stock typically responds to them.

10y contains only moves that happened in those years and knows nothing of what lies ahead today, such as an upcoming earnings date. The adaptive model does, and the Pro maps for sellers are built on it. Both describe how often a move happens, not the direction the stock will take.

Where the implied and realized lines diverge, the option market is pricing a move the stock's own behavior does not support, or the other way round.

Expiry badges

The badge above each expiry shows that expiry's overall implied volatility. The ratio below compares it with the stock's recent realized volatility.

The maps

Each map shows one value per listed contract as a heatmap on the price chart.

  • Probability: the implied touch probability.
  • Implied volatility, open interest and spread: each contract's own values. On the spread map, darker means tighter.
  • Time value per day: Each option's premium spread over the days it has left, so a seller's average daily income is visible at a glance. It is an average over the remaining life; decay speeds up toward expiry.
  • Implied vs realized: The touch probability from option prices against the realized odds of the adaptive model. Blue where the options price in more than the stock itself supports (a potential opportunity for sellers), red where less.
  • Expected value: What a short option is worth at expiration on average, as a share of its premium. Blue where selling it comes out ahead on average, red where it does not. Where our backtest did not find the figure reliable, the map is veiled and the tooltip marks it as a rough guide. The tooltip also shows the minimum premium: the premium a seller would need for the expected value to stay positive, with safety margins for statistical uncertainty already built in, both in how the stock may move and in the model itself.
  • Average and tail risk per premium: What a short option can cost the seller at expiration, net of the premium, in multiples of the premium. The average risk counts how often a loss happens and how large it is on average, the tail risk looks at the bad cases. The darker the red, the more it can cost.
  • Gamma exposure (GEX): The stock dealers would have to trade for a 1% move, per strike and expiry. Blue where their hedging tends to slow moves down, red where it can speed them up. How to read it is shown in the GEX guide.

The expected value and risk maps use the mid price. A seller receives the bid, which on far out-of-the-money contracts is noticeably lower.

For each expiry and model, the contour lines mark the listed strike whose probability is closest to your target, above and below the current price.

Limits

  • Implied probabilities are risk-neutral and assume each contract's volatility stays constant until expiry, while real prices jump, especially around earnings. They are not real-world odds, which is why Pro puts the realized odds next to them.
  • 10y replays the last ten years as they happened. It does not take future events into account, such as an upcoming earnings date. The adaptive model does.
  • The realized odds learn from the stock's own past. A break without precedent in that past, such as a takeover, a new business or a crash of a kind the stock never had, is not in them.
  • The expected value and risk figures are most reliable for expiries between one week and six months, for strikes within a normal distance of the price, and in normal volatility. Outside that range the map is veiled and the tooltip marks them as rough guides.
  • Accuracy differs between groups. In our test it was lower for calls on precious-metal ETFs and for ETF puts two to six months out than for stocks.
  • The expected value and risk figures are averages over many possible outcomes. A single trade can end far from them.
  • Free data can be delayed, incomplete or wrong. The checks catch much of that, not all of it.

All probabilities are estimates. They are not forecasts and not financial advice.
