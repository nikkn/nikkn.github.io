Do most options expire worthless? Only the ones far from the price

By Nikolai Alexander · 14 November 2026 · data through 30 October 2026

“90% of options expire worthless” is one of the most repeated claims in options trading, usually as a reason to sell them. I checked it on every trading day since 1990 for the S&P 500 and since 2004 for 469 of its stocks: how often a call or a put really expired worthless, depending on how far its strike was from the price and how long it had to run.

Key findings

  • The 90% claim holds only far from the price. One and a half typical moves away, 93% of stock calls and 94% of stock puts expired worthless within 30 days. One typical move away, 83% and 87%.
  • Close to the price the claim is far off. A quarter of a typical move away, 54% of stock calls and 67% of stock puts expired worthless within 30 days. For S&P 500 calls with 90 days to run it was 39%.
  • Puts expired worthless more often than calls at the same distance, and the gap grows with the term: S&P 500, a quarter of a typical move, 90 days: 78% of puts, 39% of calls.
  • An earnings report inside the term means fewer options expire worthless: 78% of stock calls one typical move away with a report, 86% without.
  • Expiring worthless is not the same as making money. The options that did finish in the money can cost a seller more than all the others brought in.

What is a typical move?

A strike 5% away means something very different for a calm consumer stock than for a chip maker. So this study measures the distance in typical moves: how far the stock usually moves over the term, based on its swings in the three months before. For a typical S&P 500 stock and for the index, one typical move was:

What percentage of options expire worthless?

It depends almost entirely on the distance from the price. Close to the price, between 39% and 78% expired worthless, depending on side and term. One typical move away about eight to nine in ten, two typical moves away about 97 in 100.

Why do puts expire worthless more often than calls?

Because prices rose over time. A put one typical move below the price needs a fall to finish in the money, a call one typical move above needs a rise of the same size, and rises were more common. The longer the term, the more the trend adds up: for S&P 500 options a quarter of a typical move away, the share of puts that expired worthless rose from 68% at one week to 78% at 90 days, while calls fell from 54% to 39%.

An earnings report inside the term lowers the share, because the price jumps more than in the months before. In turbulent phases more stock options expired worthless, because the strikes were set with the high volatility of those weeks and the swings calmed down again.

Year by year

In falling years more calls expired worthless and fewer puts, in rising years the other way round. The share swings by more than ten points from year to year, so a single year says little.

Why the number of worthless contracts says little

Most option contracts are closed before they expire. So the share of all traded contracts that end worthless mostly describes how traders behave, not how likely a given option is to finish worthless. This study measures the second: if you hold an option to expiry, how likely is it to end worthless?

What it means for option sellers

A high share of worthless options does not make selling them profitable by itself. A stock put one typical move away expired worthless in about 87 of 100 cases, but the other cases can cost more than all the premiums together. What counts is the premium against the size of those losses. How delta relates to the chance of finishing in the money is in this study, and OptionsCone shows the probability of finishing beyond every strike, next to what the stock itself supports.

Data and notes

  • 469 stocks of today's S&P 500 from January 2004 and the S&P 500 index (^GSPC) from June 1989, daily closes from Yahoo Finance, data through 30 October 2026. Today's index members only, so companies that left the index are missing.
  • Every trading day is a start date. Strikes are set from the close of that day, one typical move being the standard deviation of daily moves over the 63 trading days before, scaled to the term. Terms in trading days: 5, 21, 31, 42 and 63.
  • Worthless means the close on the last day was on the out-of-the-money side of the strike: at or below it for a call, at or above it for a put. Closing prices without dividends, as option strikes are.
  • Market phases: volatility of the last 21 trading days against the last 252, calm below 0.8, turbulent above 1.3. Earnings: a report between the start date and the last day, reports after the close counted on the next trading day.
  • 90% ranges from resampling whole half-years of start dates, 1,000 times, so overlapping terms are taken into account.
  • All numbers count what happened in the past. They are not probabilities for the future, not forecasts and not financial advice.
