Rolling the untested side of a strangle: which roll pays

By Nikolai Alexander · 8 November 2026 · data through 30 October 2026 · part 2 of 2

Part 1 showed that after one strike of a short strangle is touched, prices move more in both directions, and that the untested strike needs to be rolled with a fast volatility estimate to keep its risk normal. Rolling closer to the price collects more premium. Here I priced the options to see whether that premium paid for the risk, in four stock ETFs and in gold and silver.

Key findings

  • In SPY, QQQ, IWM and XLP, rolling the put up after the call was touched added about 12% of the strangle's original credit at 0.5 and 1 standard deviation. The result was positive under every pricing assumption I tested and in each of the four ETFs.
  • Rolling the call down after the put was touched only paid if the new call could be sold at a high implied volatility. For SPY with the VIX at 20, a call at 1 standard deviation had to be sold at an implied volatility of at least 18.4% (92% of the VIX), and at 0.5 standard deviations at 29.6% (148%). Out-of-the-money index calls usually trade below the VIX level.
  • Further out the two rolls swap places: at 1.5 and 2 standard deviations the call roll broke even at 66% to 81% of the index level, while the put roll after a 2 standard deviation rally lost 13% of the credit on average (from only 1,480 touches).
  • Neither roll changed the worst outcomes, which came from the tested strike. In gold and silver both rolls paid at 0.5 to 1.5 standard deviations.

How the options are priced

There is no long public history of ETF option prices to test against, so I priced every option with Black-Scholes and used each ETF's 30-day volatility index from CBOE as the implied volatility: the VIX for SPY, VXN for QQQ, RVX for IWM, GVZ for GLD and VXSLV for SLV. XLP has no index. As a second version for all six ETFs, including the years before the indices existed, I used the ETF's realized volatility scaled by its typical ratio to the index (1.16 to 1.25).

The setup follows part 1: a short strangle opened on every trading day with 30 to 90 days to expiration and strikes at 0.5 to 2 standard deviations (SD below). When one strike is touched, the untested option is bought back at the close of that day and a new one is sold at the same number of standard deviations from the new price, measured with a 10-day volatility. Both positions are held to expiration. The result of the roll is the extra premium (new option sold minus old option bought back) minus the extra payout at expiration, as a share of the strangle's original credit. For a strangle opened for a $3.00 credit, a result of 10% means the roll added $0.30 on average.

At 0.5 and 1 SD the put roll added 12.4% and 11.6% of the credit with the index level as volatility, and between 10% and 17% in the other versions. The new put kept 37.5% of its premium at 1 SD. A put sold on any day at the same distance and with the same time left kept 43.6%, so the rolled put carried somewhat more risk than usual, and its premium still covered it.

At 1.5 SD the result is unclear (4.5%, interval −5.9% to 12.0%). At 2 SD it was negative in every version, −13.3% with the index level (interval −41.4% to 6.2%), from 1,480 touches in 58 calendar quarters. A call touched at 2 SD usually means a sharp rally inside a volatile market, and after rallies the reversal was larger than usual. At 1 SD the expected payout at the rolled put was 2.03 times the payout at the same distance further up (interval 1.34 to 3.02), against 1.26 on any day. After put touches the same comparison could not be told apart from normal (0.98 against 0.80, interval 0.57 to 1.87).

Rolling the call down after a drop

For the call roll the answer depends on the price of the call, and a volatility index is a poor guide to the price of one strike. The implied volatility of each strike, and with it the skew of a stock's or ETF's options, can be read in the implied volatility map of OptionsCone. The indices are computed from a strip of out-of-the-money options and sit above at-the-money volatility (see the VIX term structure study), and on index options calls further out usually trade below at-the-money volatility while puts trade above it. Instead of relying on an assumed skew, I asked how high the implied volatility of the rolled option had to be for the roll to break even.

I state that break-even as a share of the ETF's volatility index on the day of the touch (the VIX for SPY, VXN for QQQ, RVX for IWM). A break-even of 92% means: with the VIX at 20, the roll paid on average if the new call could be sold at an implied volatility of 18.4% or more, and lost if it traded lower. The option bought back is priced at the same implied volatility. A break-even below 100% means the roll pays even when the option trades below the index level; above 100%, the option has to trade above it.

For the put roll the break-even was 62% of the index at 0.5 SD and 77% at 1 SD. Out-of-the-money puts on stock indices usually trade at or above the index level, so the put roll cleared that easily. For the call roll the break-even was 148% of the index at 0.5 SD and 92% at 1 SD (SPY alone: 87%, interval 76% to 100%). Out-of-the-money calls usually trade below the index level, and after a fall, when the index is high, the gap tends to be wider, so calls rarely reached those levels. With the call priced at exactly the index level (100%), the roll at 1 SD added 5.1% (interval −3.5% to 12.2%), with the skew version it lost 9.7%. The rolled call kept 34.1% of its premium, against 48.1% for a call sold on any day.

At 1.5 and 2 SD the call roll broke even at only 81% and 66% of the index level. With the index level it added 15.5% and 21.0% of the credit, with the skew version −5.7% and 1.9%, so the outcome depends on how cheap far calls were at the time. In this sample a fall large enough to touch a put at 2 SD was rarely followed by a rally all the way back to a call rolled that far out within the term.

By ETF

The put roll was positive in each stock ETF, from 8.6% of the credit in IWM to 12.8% in SPY, with intervals above zero. The call roll ranged from 2.4% in XLP to 7.6% in SPY, and every one of its intervals reaches zero or below, even with the index level as volatility, which flatters the call.

What the roll does not change

Among strangles at 1 SD whose put was touched, the worst 5% lost 4.39 times the original credit when held without a roll and 4.09 times with the call roll. After a call touch, the worst 5% lost 2.22 times the credit without and 2.10 times with the put roll. In those cases the loss came from the tested strike, and the untested side is too small to offset it. On average, touched strangles at 1 SD held to expiration kept 3.8% of their credit after a put touch and 16.7% after a call touch, with the index level as volatility.

In GLD and SLV the risk after a touch sat on the tested side. After a call touch the same distance further up was reached in 40% of cases (35% normally), while the rolled put was touched in 24% (27% normally), and the payout ratio was 0.38 against 0.63. In the stock ETFs it was the other way round after call touches: 1.63 against 1.19.

With option prices from GVZ and VXSLV (available from 2008 and 2011), both rolls paid at 1 SD: the put roll after a rally added 18.3% of the credit (interval 10.4% to 25.7%), the call roll after a fall 12.5% (5.5% to 18.1%). The break-even was between 76% and 83% of the index level on either side up to 1.5 SD. Two ETFs, overlapping windows and 15 to 18 years of index data make this the weakest part of the study.

What it means for strangle sellers

In the stock ETFs, rolling the put up after a rally paid at 0.5 and 1 SD under every assumption, while the version at 2 SD lost on average. Rolling the call down after a fall paid only if the new call could be sold near the full volatility index, a price index calls rarely reach after a sell-off; at 1.5 and 2 SD the threshold was lower, and whether it was met depended on the skew. In gold and silver both rolls paid. In every case the roll was a small addition to the average result, and the outcome of a touched strangle was decided by the tested strike.

Data and notes

  • Prices: daily data of SPY, QQQ, IWM, XLP, GLD and SLV through 30 October 2026 as in part 1. Volatility indices: CBOE daily closes of VIX, VXN, RVX, GVZ and VXSLV.
  • Options are modeled, not observed: Black-Scholes with rates and dividends set to zero (forward = spot), time in trading days / 252, and the 30-day index used for every term. After shocks short-term implied volatility usually trades above the 30-day index, and in normal markets longer terms trade above it.
  • An index level is not the implied volatility of a specific strike. For that reason the main result is stated as the break-even level. The skew version is an assumption, not measured data.
  • Realized vol version: m × √(0.5 × 10-day² + 0.5 × 42-day²) volatility, with m the median ratio of the index to that blend: SPY 1.25, QQQ 1.18, IWM 1.18, GLD 1.16, SLV 1.16; XLP 1.20, the average of the three stock ETFs.
  • Roll: at the close of the touch day, untested option bought back, new option sold at the same number of standard deviations from that close with a 10-day volatility, same expiration, only toward the price (if the new distance were further out, the old strike is kept, in under 2% of cases at 1 SD), held to expiration. Trading costs are not included; a roll pays two bid-ask spreads.
  • Normal level for the premium kept: the same option (same distance, same days left, same pricing) sold on every trading day.
  • Intervals: 90%, from 2,000 resamples of whole calendar quarters of the touch date.
  • All numbers count what happened in the past. They are not probabilities for the future, not forecasts and not financial advice.
