Is delta the probability of expiring in the money?

Nikolai Alexander · 13 October 2026

Many traders read an option's delta as its chance of finishing in the money. I checked how far that holds: first in the textbook model, then in four real option chains, SPY, Apple, Broadcom and Bloom Energy.

Key findings

  • In the textbook model, the gap between delta and the probability grows with volatility and time. At 80% implied volatility and six months, a 10-delta put finishes in the money 24% of the time, a 10-delta call 3%.
  • In real chains, the skew changes the picture. On SPY, a 30-delta put has only a 22% chance of finishing in the money and a 30-delta call 33%: the opposite of the textbook.
  • On a volatile stock with a flat skew, the textbook gap stays. On Bloom Energy, a 30-delta put has a 43% chance, a 30-delta call 18%.
  • Delta works as odds only where the two effects roughly cancel, as they did for Apple.

The rule of thumb

Delta measures how much an option's price moves when the stock moves by one dollar. Because it runs from 0 to 1 for calls (and 0 to −1 for puts), it is often read as the probability of the option finishing in the money: a 30-delta put, about a 30% chance. Many broker platforms show a separate "probability in the money" next to delta, and for short-dated options the two are usually within a couple of points.

The question is how well that holds for longer expiries, for volatile stocks, and once the real shape of the option chain is taken into account.

In the textbook model, the gap grows with volatility and time

The textbook model (Black-Scholes) uses one volatility for every strike. A call's delta is N(d1), but its probability of finishing in the money is N(d2), with d2 = d1 − σ√T. The two are only equal when volatility times the square root of time is close to zero. So delta and the probability are different numbers by construction, a mix-up many traders make.

They drift apart the higher the volatility and the longer the time to expiry. For puts, the probability ends up above delta; for calls, below it.

At 20% volatility and 30 days, the rule of thumb works: a 30-delta put finishes in the money 32% of the time. At 80% volatility and six months, the same 30-delta put does so 51% of the time, and a 30-delta call only 14%.

In real option chains, the skew changes the picture

In a real option chain, each strike trades at its own implied volatility. On most stocks and especially on indexes, out-of-the-money puts carry a higher volatility than calls: the skew. That changes the odds in two ways, so I compare three numbers for each contract:

  • Delta, as usual.
  • Without skew: the probability with each strike's own implied volatility in the textbook formula, which ignores how the volatility changes from strike to strike. This is what broker platforms usually show.
  • With skew: the probability implied by the whole chain, which also takes into account how the volatility changes from strike to strike.

The four stocks fall into three groups:

  • SPY, steep skew: the skew more than reverses the textbook effect. A 30-delta put has a 22% chance of finishing in the money, below its delta, and a 30-delta call 33%, above it. For index options, delta overstates the chance of a put finishing in the money.
  • Apple, moderate skew: the skew roughly cancels the volatility effect. With skew, a 30-delta put is at 30% and a 30-delta call at 28%, close to delta.
  • Broadcom and Bloom Energy, flat skew: the skew changes little, and the textbook gap remains. On Bloom Energy, at about 70% implied volatility, a 30-delta put has a 43% chance of finishing in the money and a 30-delta call 18%.

Not even an option with a delta of 0.50 is a coin toss: with skew, the 0.50-delta put is at 44% on SPY and 64% on Bloom Energy.

What it means for traders

The same delta stands for very different odds, depending on two properties of the stock: how steep its skew is, and how high its volatility is. A steep skew pushes the odds of puts below delta and those of calls above it. High volatility and long expiries push them the other way. Delta is a reasonable stand-in for the probability only where these two effects roughly cancel, and the broker-style probability with each strike's own volatility does not show the skew effect at all.

For underlyings with relatively low implied volatility, a moderate skew and short expiries, delta is a good approximation of the probability of finishing in the money. The steeper the skew, the higher the volatility and the longer the expiry, the less accurate it becomes. Whether delta then overstates or understates the probability depends on which of the two effects is stronger:

SkewVolatility and timePuts: deltaCalls: delta
FlatLow volatility, short expiryabout rightabout right
ModerateModerate volatilityabout rightabout right
FlatHigh volatility or long expiryunderstatesoverstates
SteepLow volatilityoverstatesunderstates

"Overstates" means the probability of finishing in the money is lower than delta suggests; "understates" means it is higher. With a steep skew and high volatility at the same time, the two effects work against each other, and which one wins depends on the stock.

Traders who read delta as odds, for example when choosing which strikes to sell, need to be aware of that gap.

Data and notes

  • Option chains for SPY, AAPL, AVGO and BE from Yahoo Finance, downloaded with OptionsCone on 12 October 2026 during US trading hours. Expiries 13 November 2026 (32 days), 15 January 2027 (95 days) and 19 March 2027 (158 days); for SPY the longest is 31 March 2027 (170 days).
  • Interest rate 4.05% (13-week US Treasury bill). Deltas are model deltas without dividends; a broker's deltas can differ slightly.
  • The probabilities with skew were confirmed against the raw option prices of neighboring strikes: typically within about 1 percentage point, on Bloom Energy within about 2.5 points because of its wider bid-ask spreads.
  • The textbook values were confirmed by a simulation of 400,000 price paths.
  • All probabilities are risk-neutral: they describe what option prices imply, including the market's price for risk. They are not forecasts and not financial advice.
