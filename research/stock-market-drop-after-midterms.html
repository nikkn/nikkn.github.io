Is the stock market crashing? The cycle’s most reliable stretch just had its worst start since 1974

By Nikolai Alexander · 21 November 2026 · data through 20 November 2026

If you searched “is the stock market crashing” this week, you are not alone: it is one of the first things Google suggests right now. The S&P 500 is 11.6% below its October high. What makes this drop unusual is its timing: we are in the post-midterm phase, the stretch from the midterm election to the end of the following year, historically the most reliable part of the four-year presidential cycle. Here is what happened when that phase started with a drop before.

Key points

  • We are in the post-midterm phase, historically the most reliable stretch of the presidential cycle: since 1950 the S&P 500 ended it higher every time, 19 of 19, by a median of 20%.
  • This one started with a drop: −8.7% in the 13 trading days since the election on 3 November, and 11.6% below the October high. That is a correction (10%), not a bear market (20%). Only 1974 started worse.
  • AI and chip stocks fell much harder: the semiconductor index is 42% below its June high, the Nasdaq-100 20% below its October high. More on that in a separate note.
  • Six post-midterm phases fell 7% or more along the way, four of them deeper than today. In all six, the index was back above its election-day level within eight weeks of the low.
  • The three deep drops that came early in the phase (1974, 2002, 2018) ended it 17% to 22% higher. The weak endings came from drops late in the phase, with little time left to recover.

Where we are now

On election day, 3 November, the S&P 500 closed at 7,569. On Friday, 20 November, it closed at 6,910, after a fall of 3.7% on that day alone. That is 8.7% below election day and 11.6% below the all-time high of 7,819 on 6 October.

The index hides how uneven the drop is. AI and chip stocks fell far harder: the semiconductor index is down 21% since election day and 42% from its June high, Nvidia 47% from its October high, and the Nasdaq-100 20%, right at the line usually called a bear market. The average S&P 500 stock, with every company weighted equally, is only 7% below its high. That deserves its own note, which will follow.

Why the timing matters

The presidential cycle is one of the oldest seasonal patterns in the stock market: weak first and second years, a typical low around the midterm election, then the strongest stretch of the cycle. From the midterm election to the end of the following year, the post-midterm phase, the S&P 500 rose in all 19 cycles since 1950, by a median of 20%.

That is why this drop stands out: it hit the most reliable stretch of the cycle right at its start. It also starts from high up. Measured over the whole cycle, the current one is still up 16.3% since January 2025. The average cycle stood at +10.8% at this point, and 12 of the 19 past cycles were lower here than this one is today.

The full study of the cycle, with every cycle since 1949 and how strong starts compared, is in the S&P 500 before the midterms.

Has the market started this badly before?

The chart shows the first four months of every post-midterm phase since 1950, each line starting at 0% on election day.

In the same 13 trading days, only 1974 fell further (−9.6%). In 2018 the drop was as deep in the end (−14.7% by 24 December), but it took seven weeks, and in 2002 it took four months. In both 1974 and 2018 the index was back above its election-day level about three months after the election.

How deep past drops went, and where they ended

For each post-midterm phase since 1950: the lowest close below election day (gray), and where the index stood at the end of the phase (blue). Both are measured from the close on election day.

Every gray bar has a blue one next to it. Small dips were the rule: in 13 of 19 phases the index never closed more than 5% below election day.

The deep drops: deep at times, but short

Six post-midterm phases closed 7% or more below election day at some point. Each panel shows one of them, from election day to the end of the phase, with 2026 so far in orange.

Of course, the low is only known in hindsight. Nobody knew on 6 December 1974 or on 24 December 2018 that the bottom was in. But once it was in, the index was back above its election-day level within eight weeks in all six phases, in 2010 within a week. Corrections in the post-midterm phase could be deep, but they did not last long.

When the low came made the difference for how the phase ended. The three drops that came early, within four months of the election (1974, 2002, 2018), ended the phase 17% to 22% higher. The three that came late, 10 to 13 months after the election (1986, 2010, 2014), ended it only 0.4% to 5.4% higher. That is partly mechanical: a late low leaves little time to recover before the end of the year. With three cases on each side, it is a description, not a rule. So far, 2026 is an early drop. Whether it stays one is only known later.

1986 is the warning case: the index rose strongly into the summer of 1987, then fell 33.5% from its August peak to early December, through the crash of October 1987, and ended the phase just 0.4% above election day.

What this means, and what it does not

  • A base rate, not a forecast. Drops like this have happened in the post-midterm phase before, and in none of the 19 cycles did the market stay below its election-day level through the end of the phase. That does not mean it cannot happen this time.
  • 19 is a small number. A clean 19 out of 19 still fits a true rate as low as 82% (95% confidence interval: 82% to 100%). Almost one cycle in five could end lower without contradicting history.
  • The calendar does not know the news. The cycle is a pattern in the calendar. It says nothing about interest rates, earnings or how richly AI stocks are valued, and those can matter more than the date.

Data and notes

  • S&P 500 price index, daily closes since 1950, data through 20 November 2026. Price index without dividends.
  • AI and chips: the semiconductor index that SOXL tracks, the Nasdaq-100, Nvidia, and the equal-weighted S&P 500 (RSP), closes through 20 November 2026.
  • Post-midterm phase: from the close on midterm election day to the last close of the following year, about 14 months. Election day is the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. Until 1980 the exchange was closed on election days, so the last close before it is used.
  • “Deepest fall” is the lowest close below the election-day close up to the last close of the following year. “Back above election day” is the first close at or above it after that low, counted in calendar days from the low.
  • Correction (10% below the high) and bear market (20%) are conventions, not official thresholds. The 95% interval is the exact binomial (Clopper–Pearson) interval for 19 of 19. Early and late drops: the lowest close came within four months of the election, or 10 to 13 months after it. Comparing all deep drops with the other phases instead depends on where “deep” starts: with a 5% to 7% line the deep ones ended lower (median +11% vs +23%, p = 0.03), with 8% or more there is no gap, so the page does not use that comparison.
  • All numbers count what happened in past cycles. They are not probabilities for the future, not forecasts and not financial advice.
